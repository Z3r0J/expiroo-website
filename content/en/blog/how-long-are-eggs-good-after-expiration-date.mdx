**Eggs kept in the fridge the whole time are often still good after the "sell by" or "EXP" date, because on a USDA-graded carton that date is a store deadline set no more than 30 days after packing.** The American Egg Board says refrigerated eggs keep their quality for at least 4 to 5 weeks after the pack date, so the pack date printed on the carton tells you more than the sell-by date does.

The FDA is a little stricter: it says to use eggs within 3 weeks for best quality. Below is how to find the pack date, what each date on the carton means, and when to throw eggs out no matter what the date says.

## Short answer

| Situation | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Past a "sell by" or "EXP" date, kept refrigerated | Often still fine. Count from the pack date instead: quality holds at least 4 to 5 weeks after packing ([American Egg Board](https://www.incredibleegg.org/egg-handling-storage-tips/)) |
| Past a "best before" or "use by" date | That date already marks the end of the expected quality window ([USDA AMS](https://www.ams.usda.gov/sites/default/files/media/Egg%20Grading%20Manual.pdf)). Use them up soon, in fully cooked dishes |
| No pack date you can find | Use them within 3 weeks of buying them ([American Egg Board](https://www.incredibleegg.org/egg-handling-storage-tips/), [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/what-you-need-know-about-egg-safety)) |
| Left out of the fridge more than 2 hours | Throw them out ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/what-you-need-know-about-egg-safety)) |
| Cracked, slimy, moldy, or an unusual smell or color inside | Throw them out ([American Egg Board](https://www.incredibleegg.org/egg-handling-storage-tips/)) |
| Hard-boiled | Within 1 week ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/what-you-need-know-about-egg-safety)) |

Clemson University's extension service gives a slightly wider window for raw eggs: use them within three to five weeks of putting them in the fridge ([Clemson HGIC](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/safe-handling-of-eggs/)). If you want one number to remember, 3 weeks from purchase is the cautious one.

## Reading the carton

A US egg carton can carry up to three different dates, and only one of them is about how old the eggs are.

**The pack date (a three-digit code).** On cartons with the USDA grade shield, the day the eggs were packed has to be on the package, written as the day of the year ([USDA AMS](https://www.ams.usda.gov/sites/default/files/media/Egg%20Grading%20Manual.pdf)). Look for a three-digit number on the side of the carton: 001 is January 1, 049 is February 18 and 198 is July 17 ([USDA AMS](https://www.ams.usda.gov/grades-standards/shell-egg-day-year-chart)). The American Egg Board's example: eggs packed on June 15 are marked 166.

**"Sell by" or "EXP".** These dates are optional. When a packer uses one, it can't be more than 30 days after the pack date, counting the pack date itself ([USDA AMS](https://www.ams.usda.gov/sites/default/files/media/Egg%20Grading%20Manual.pdf)). It tells the store when to stop selling the eggs, not when you have to stop eating them ([American Egg Board](https://www.incredibleegg.org/egg-handling-storage-tips/)). The packer can also pick a shorter window, so two cartons packed the same day can carry different sell-by dates.

**"Best before" or "use by".** These phrases mark the end of the expected quality window, and they can be up to 45 days after the pack date ([USDA AMS](https://www.ams.usda.gov/sites/default/files/media/Egg%20Grading%20Manual.pdf)). If your carton uses one of these instead of a sell-by date, you've already been given the longer window, so be less generous once it passes.

**How to use it.** Find the three-digit code, work out the pack date, and count 4 to 5 weeks from there. A carton marked 300 was packed on October 27 (October 26 in a leap year), so its quality window runs to roughly late November or early December, whatever the sell-by date says.

For the wider picture of what "sell by", "best by" and "use by" mean on other foods, see our guide to [best before vs use by](/en/blog/best-before-vs-use-by).

## Float and smell test (what it does and doesn't tell you)

The float test checks the air cell, a pocket of air at the wide end of the egg. As an egg ages, moisture and carbon dioxide leave through the shell and air comes in, so the air cell grows ([American Egg Board](https://www.incredibleegg.org/egg-handling-storage-tips/)). A bigger air cell makes an egg more buoyant.

What the float test can't do:

- **It can't date an egg reliably.** The American Egg Board notes that a very fresh egg can have a large air cell and float anyway. In salt water the test is meaningless: add enough salt and every egg floats.
- **It can't tell you an egg is safe.** Salmonella can be inside eggs with clean, uncracked shells, including fresh ones ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/what-you-need-know-about-egg-safety)). An egg that sinks can still carry it, and you can't see bacteria with the naked eye ([American Egg Board](https://www.incredibleegg.org/egg-handling-storage-tips/)).

The smell test works in one direction only. Crack the egg into a separate bowl first. Throw it out if it smells unusual, if the white or yolk has an odd color, or if the shell is slimy or has powdery spots, which can mean mold ([American Egg Board](https://www.incredibleegg.org/egg-handling-storage-tips/)). An egg that smells fine still needs to be cooked properly.

## Fridge door vs shelf

Keep eggs in their original carton on a shelf in the main part of the fridge, not in the door. The door is the warmest spot and its temperature swings every time you open it ([American Egg Board](https://www.incredibleegg.org/egg-handling-storage-tips/)). Clemson's extension service says the same: store them in the grocery carton in the coldest part of the refrigerator, not the door ([Clemson HGIC](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/safe-handling-of-eggs/)).

- **Fridge temperature:** 40°F (4°C) or below. A fridge thermometer is the only way to know ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/what-you-need-know-about-egg-safety)).
- **Keep the carton.** The FDA recommends storing eggs in it, and the pack date stays with the eggs that way.
- **Don't wash them.** US eggs are washed before packing, and washing at home can help bacteria on the shell get inside ([Clemson HGIC](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/safe-handling-of-eggs/)).
- **Don't freeze them in the shell.** Beat the yolks and whites together first, then freeze and use within a year ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/what-you-need-know-about-egg-safety)).

## Hard-boiled eggs

Hard-boiled eggs don't last as long as raw ones. Use them within 1 week of cooking, in the shell or peeled ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/what-you-need-know-about-egg-safety)). Cooking washes away the protective coating on the shell, so refrigerate them within two hours of cooking ([Clemson HGIC](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/safe-handling-of-eggs/)).

The American Egg Board is stricter about peeled eggs: keep hard-boiled eggs in their shells in a loosely covered container (not the original carton), and eat them the same day once peeled. Don't freeze whole hard-boiled eggs or whites; they turn tough and watery.

Cooked egg dishes, like quiche or a frittata, keep 3 to 4 days in the fridge ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/what-you-need-know-about-egg-safety)).

## Who should be stricter

Most people who get Salmonella recover in 4 to 7 days without treatment, but some get sick enough to need a hospital. The FDA lists the people at greater risk of severe illness: children, older adults, pregnant women and people with weakened immune systems, such as transplant patients and people with HIV/AIDS, cancer or diabetes ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/what-you-need-know-about-egg-safety)).

If you're cooking for someone in that group:

- **Skip the generous end of the window.** Use the 3-weeks-from-purchase rule, not the pack-date maximum.
- **Cook eggs until the yolk and white are firm.** People at risk should avoid soft-cooked or runny eggs ([Clemson HGIC](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/safe-handling-of-eggs/)).
- **Put older eggs into fully cooked food.** As eggs age, the yolk membrane weakens, which makes it easier for bacteria to reach the yolk. The American Egg Board suggests using older eggs in things like quiches and baked goods.
- **Use pasteurized eggs for raw recipes** like Caesar dressing or homemade ice cream ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/what-you-need-know-about-egg-safety)).

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you eat eggs after the sell-by date?

Usually, if they've been refrigerated the whole time and look and smell normal. On USDA-graded eggs the sell-by date is at most 30 days after packing, and refrigerated eggs keep their quality for at least 4 to 5 weeks after the pack date. Cook them thoroughly.

### What does the three-digit number on an egg carton mean?

It's the pack date written as the day of the year: 001 is January 1 and 365 is December 31. Count 4 to 5 weeks from that date for quality.

### Is the float test reliable?

No. It hints at the size of the air cell, but a very fresh egg can float, and the test says nothing about Salmonella. Crack the egg into a separate bowl and check how it looks and smells instead.

### How long do hard-boiled eggs last in the fridge?

One week, according to the FDA. Refrigerate them within two hours of cooking.

### Should eggs go in the fridge door?

No. Keep them in their carton on a shelf in the main compartment, where the temperature is colder and steadier.
